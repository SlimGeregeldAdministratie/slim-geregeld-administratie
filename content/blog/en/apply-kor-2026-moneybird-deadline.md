You apply for the Dutch small businesses scheme (kleineondernemersregeling, KOR) through the Tax Administration’s business portal (Mijn Belastingdienst Zakelijk), and its [application page lists 3 December](https://www.belastingdienst.nl/wps/wcm/connect/nl/btw/content/aanmelden-kor) as the deadline for a 1 January start. For a January 2027 start, use that published date when planning your application in 2026. Separate the application decision from the bookkeeping work you want to do in Moneybird. Before changing your approach, calculate how much value added tax (btw) on business purchases would become a cost under KOR.

## Should you decide about KOR before changing Moneybird?

Yes, decide whether the numbers work before treating KOR as a software task. Several people in recent months have asked me whether KOR suits their business. My starting point in those conversations is the VAT on their expenses, because that amount can disappear from the comparison when the discussion focuses only on low turnover.

The useful question is how much your purchases actually cost after any recoverable VAT. A business with modest sales can still have expenses carrying VAT, and the practice pattern I see includes precisely that combination. Looking only at the sales total leaves an important part of the calculation unfinished.

For your Moneybird preparation, keep the decision and its implementation as separate questions. First establish the financial effect you are accepting. Then use that decision as the basis for how you want the bookkeeping to be handled once you are clear about participating.

## What should you compare before applying?

Compare business expenses with recoverable supplier VAT against those same expenses when that VAT becomes a cost. In an ordinary [VAT return (btw-aangifte)](https://www.belastingdienst.nl/wps/wcm/connect/bldcontentnl/belastingdienst/zakelijk/btw/btw_aangifte_doen_en_betalen/), you report VAT charged to customers and deductible VAT, with the difference determining the amount payable or refundable. For the decision below, I isolate the supplier side so you can see the cost effect clearly.

The worked example uses EUR 10,000 in sales revenue and EUR 1,000 in purchases before VAT. Assume those supplier invoices show EUR 200 VAT in total; this is an example amount, with no single VAT rate assumed. Sales revenue stays the same in both columns, so the comparison shows what happens when that EUR 200 can no longer be reclaimed.

This pattern comes up in several files. A worked example with round amounts:

| Item | Without KOR, supplier VAT recoverable | Under KOR |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sales revenue used in the example | EUR 10,000 | EUR 10,000 |
| Purchases before supplier VAT | EUR 1,000 | EUR 1,000 |
| VAT shown on supplier invoices | EUR 200 | EUR 200 |
| Supplier VAT reclaimed | EUR 200 | EUR 0 |
| Purchases remaining as a cost | EUR 1,000 | EUR 1,200 |
| Revenue less these costs | EUR 9,000 | EUR 8,800 |

The example leaves EUR 8,800 after these costs under KOR, compared with EUR 9,000 where the supplier VAT is recoverable. The difference follows directly from the EUR 200 on the purchase invoices. That is the amount to recognise in your comparison before deciding whether the scheme suits your business.

## When would you pause the Moneybird change?

Pause when your reason for choosing KOR is simply that your turnover looks low. That reasoning leaves the expense side unanswered. In the example, purchases still require EUR 1,200 in cash, and losing the EUR 200 recovery changes the cost you carry.

A useful comparison starts with the actual invoices behind your expenses. Put the purchase amount and its VAT alongside each other, then calculate the total cost you would retain under KOR. This gives you a specific amount to weigh instead of an assumption that a small business automatically benefits financially.

There is also a practical difference between understanding the calculation and being ready to implement it. If you cannot explain where the supplier VAT ends up in your comparison, leave the accounting change pending. Resolve that amount first so the treatment in your records follows a decision you understand.

## What bookkeeping problem can distort the comparison?

Unclassified sales can make the figures you use for the decision unreliable. In several sets of accounts, sales had been left as uncategorised income, which meant the profit and loss statement was wrong until the entries were classified. That is a recurring bookkeeping error I would address before relying on the displayed result.

For this decision, I would check the entries behind the sales and expense totals rather than judge the business from one headline figure. The table works because its assumptions are visible and the supplier VAT is shown separately. Your own comparison needs that same clarity, especially where a payment has been entered without a useful classification.

## What should you do before the December deadline?

Prepare a supplier VAT total before moving from consideration to application. Use the purchase invoices you expect to include in your comparison and show the VAT separately from the underlying costs. You can then see the amount that would become an expense under KOR.

My message is clear: make the supplier VAT calculation before changing your Moneybird bookkeeping for KOR. Start by adding up the VAT on the purchase invoices in your comparison. Enter that total as an additional cost in your KOR column.
