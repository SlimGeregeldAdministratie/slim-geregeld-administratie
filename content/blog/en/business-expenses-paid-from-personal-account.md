You can record demonstrably business-related expenses you paid personally before opening the business account for your sole proprietorship (eenmanszaak). Keep the receipts and invoices, record the appropriate business expense, and use a private capital contribution (privéstorting) as the corresponding entry. The deductible expense reduces your profit; the contribution itself gives you no additional tax deduction. Mixed use, deduction restrictions, depreciation and the conditions for reclaiming value added tax (btw) still need separate attention.

## Can you record costs paid before your business account existed?

Yes, personally paid business costs can belong in your business records even when the business account was not yet open. Several people in recent months have asked me about business costs they paid personally before their business account existed. The recurring purchases in these questions include equipment, software and registration costs. My starting point is to establish what the purchase was for and retain the supporting receipt or invoice.

In those files, I record the business expenditure with a corresponding capital contribution. That records both the purchase and the personal funding behind it. The [Dutch Tax Administration explains that private contributions increase business capital](https://www.belastingdienst.nl/wps/wcm/connect/bldcontentnl/belastingdienst/zakelijk/winst/inkomstenbelasting/inkomstenbelasting_voor_ondernemers/privestortingen_en_priveonttrekkingen) and are excluded from business profit. This treatment concerns a sole proprietorship; it should not automatically be carried over to another business structure.

## How do you approach the entry in Moneybird?

For your Moneybird records, separate the purchase from the way you funded it. Start with the receipt or invoice and identify the business expenditure it supports. Then record the personal funding through the capital contribution entry. The practical question is whether the records show both sides of what happened.

When explaining this to people starting their administration, I keep the purchase document and the funding entry together in the discussion. For example, software paid from a personal account still needs to be assessed as software expenditure. The contribution explains how you financed that purchase. It does not decide whether the expenditure qualifies for immediate deduction.

That distinction matters when you look at your reports. A [profit and loss statement (winst-en-verliesrekening)](https://www.kvk.nl/producten-bestellen/welke-gegevens-staan-er-in-een-jaarrekening/) shows business revenue and costs over a year, while a balance sheet (balans) shows the business position at a particular moment. Your expense and your personal funding therefore answer different accounting questions. Keep that distinction in mind when checking the completed entry.

## What does a privately funded expense look like in figures?

A privately funded deductible expense has an expense side and a matching funding side. For the example below, assume the equipment and software expenditure qualifies fully as a current business expense. Any depreciation, deduction restriction or separate VAT treatment must be assessed before applying that assumption to your own purchases.

This pattern comes up in several files. A worked example with round amounts:

| Part of the example | Amount | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Qualifying equipment and software expense paid personally | EUR 1,000 | Recorded as business expenditure |
| Private capital contribution recorded alongside the expense | EUR 1,000 | Records your personal funding |
| Reduction in profit from the deductible expense | EUR 1,000 | Comes from the expense entry |

The matching amounts make the distinction visible. You have funded the purchase personally, and the deductible expenditure reduces profit by the amount shown. There is no additional deduction for supplying that money yourself. When reviewing the entry, check that you can explain the expense and the contribution independently.

## Which parts of the purchase need a separate assessment?

The deductible amount depends on the business use and the treatment of the expenditure. With mixed business and personal use, only the business portion is deductible. Some purchases are subject to deduction restrictions or depreciation. Paying from a personal account does not remove those distinctions.

Equipment deserves particular attention before you copy the example into your records. The example assumes an expense that can be deducted immediately, so first establish whether that treatment fits the actual purchase. Keep the invoice available while making that assessment. The same discipline applies when a receipt covers items with different uses.

VAT recovery also needs its own assessment. Your [VAT return (btw-aangifte)](https://www.belastingdienst.nl/wps/wcm/connect/bldcontentnl/belastingdienst/zakelijk/btw/btw_aangifte_doen_en_betalen/) includes the VAT charged to customers and the VAT you are entitled to deduct. A capital contribution entry does not establish that entitlement. Reclaim VAT only where the applicable conditions are met.

## What should you do with those early receipts now?

Bring the personally paid purchases into your bookkeeping review rather than leaving them outside the records because of the account used. For each purchase, keep the supporting document and establish the business portion and the appropriate expense treatment. Then connect the qualifying expenditure to the personal funding through the capital contribution. This is also a useful category to include when collecting your [year-end documents](/en/blog/sole-trader-year-end-documents/).

My advice: record demonstrably business-related early expenditure in your sole proprietorship with the corresponding private capital contribution. Assess mixed use, deduction restrictions, depreciation and VAT before treating the full invoice amount as deductible. In the example, the EUR 1,000 contribution creates no additional deduction.
